This is the module that takes real time, and the one most people give up on. Do it last.

Two steps: the display tells you which button to **read**, and that button's label tells you
which button to **press**. The words are chosen to be maximally confusable — "LEAD", "LED"
and "LEED" all appear and all point somewhere different.

## Step 1 — which button to read

<WhosOnFirstStep1 />

Twenty-eight words to place into six positions. Grouping the ones that look alike is what
makes it tractable:

1. **The one-offs and the near-homophones.** *UR* is Top Left, alone. *C / First / Okay* are
   Top Right. *Yes / Led / Nothing* are Middle Left. Then the sound-alike families: *eed*
   (Leed, Reed) is Bottom Left, and *red* (Red, Read) plus the no-space *you* words (You,
   Your, You're — but **not** "You are") are Middle Right.
2. **They are / Their / They're form a triangle** — They are and Their side by side, They're
   below the left one. Add *There* in Bottom Right and it becomes a square.
3. **The blanks.** *Blank* and the empty display sit diagonally: Blank in Middle Right, the
   empty display in Bottom Left.
4. **Everything else is Bottom Right.** It is the biggest bucket, so make it your default and
   only learn the exceptions.

## Step 2 — which button to press

The 28 button labels split into **two groups of 14**, and a module only ever draws from one
of them. So recognising a single word tells you which 14 you are working with — that alone
halves the problem.

You also never need the whole list. **At most the first nine words**, and you can stop as
soon as you reach the label's own word, because the label is always one of the six buttons.
(That is not a rule of thumb — six buttons out of fourteen words means the first nine can
never all miss. I checked every legal module against it.)

For each list I made a sentence whose words' first letters spell the priority order, starting
with the step-1 label itself. Where a letter is ambiguous — no/nothing, ready/right,
wait/what — I used the actual word or something that sounds like it.

### Group 2

Group 2 is harder to write sentences for: four words start with **U** and four with **Y**, so
first letters alone don't disambiguate. I fell back on numbering them by position in the
alphabetised list — 1(u), 2(ur), 5(you're) — and on bracketed clusters where the order inside
doesn't matter for the words on the module. These are uglier than group 1 and that is
honestly just how it went.

## Practice

Drill the two steps separately before you put them together. They fail for different reasons:
step 1 is a lookup of near-identical words, step 2 is recall of a list. Mixing them from the
start means you can't tell which half is letting you down.
